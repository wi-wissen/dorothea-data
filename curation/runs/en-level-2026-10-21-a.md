## What happened

1. **First round.** Three of the six bulk agents (batches 00–03, 12–19) rated each row by the word as
   learners meet it. The other three (04–11, 20–23) rated the single, often rare sense shown in the
   gloss and put 40–70 % of their rows at C2. The independent repeat of batch 17 agreed with the
   first group (79 % identical, 98 % within one level); the repeat of batch 05 disagreed strongly with
   its first rating. A clarified instruction made a re-run of 08–11 even harsher, so it was dropped.
2. **Cause.** 4,575 input rows were rare parts of speech of words that already had a level (*piano*
   as an adverb, *brother* as a verb), and 2,121 were separate Wiktionary entries for inflected
   spellings (*got*, *firms*, *feels*). Both groups had to guess what such a row means.
3. **Rules** (applied to all batches, replacing agent ratings):
   - **inherited**: a headword that already has a level in another part of speech gets that level
     (the lowest one if several). A reader meets the word, not the part-of-speech entry.
   - **no level**: an entry whose spelling is an inflected form of a leveled word (from the forms
     table of the base entry, or a regular -s/-es/-ies/-ed/-ing ending) gets no level.
4. **Second round.** The 2,626 remaining new words from the twelve discarded batches were rated again
   with the original instruction (section "Second round" below), 6 agents. On lower-case words the
   two rounds then agree by frequency band (e.g. ranks above 10,000: B2/C1/C2 13/49/34 % vs.
   19/42/34 %); they still differed on capitalised headwords, so all of those went to review.
5. **Review.** The larger model checked 999 rows and changed 136 (108 up, 28 down). Most raised rows
   are capitalised entries that are not the name but a rare common-noun sense (*John* as slang for a
   toilet); letters of the alphabet were lowered to A1/A2; well-known names and brands were aligned.

## Bulk instruction (verbatim, incl. second round)

# Run en-level-2026-10-21-a: CEFR levels for English headwords

You assign a CEFR level (A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, C2) to English dictionary headwords. The level answers:
**at which stage does a learner of English as a foreign language (for example a German-speaking
school student) typically learn and need this word?** Work from your own knowledge of English and of
how English is learned. Do not look up or reproduce any published word list (exam lists, textbook
lists, vocabulary profiles), and do not open any other files than the ones named below.

## Levels (own descriptions)

- **A1** – first weeks: greetings, numbers, family, food, the home, time, the most frequent verbs and
  adjectives, and all core function words (articles, pronouns, common prepositions and conjunctions,
  question words, auxiliaries, modal verbs).
- **A2** – everyday routine: shopping, doctor, appointments, transport, school, simple work, weather,
  feelings, describing people and past events; frequent concrete nouns and common verbs.
- **B1** – independent everyday use: opinions and reasons, work and training, media, health in more
  detail, travel problems, the news in simple terms; common abstract nouns; common phrasal verbs.
- **B2** – abstract and specialised topics of general interest: society, politics, economy,
  environment, science in general terms; nuanced verbs and adjectives; formal letters.
- **C1** – precise, educated vocabulary for study and work: academic and administrative words,
  nuanced or less frequent synonyms, idiomatic and stylistically marked words.
- **C2** – rare, literary, technical, regional or old-fashioned words that even advanced learners
  meet only occasionally.

Guidance:
- Judge the word in its most common meaning (the German gloss shows which entry is meant).
- Compounds and derived words: the level of the whole word, usually not lower than its hardest part;
  transparent everyday words (bedroom, unhappy) can be as low as their parts.
- Specialist or school-subject terms (photosynthesis, equation) are usually B2 or higher unless they
  are everyday words.
- Rude, vulgar or slang words: the level at which learners understand them, typically B2+.
- Use the whole scale. Do not default to B1 when unsure; decide, and mark `unsure` instead.

## Second round

The second-round inputs are `new-N.tsv` (N = 0–5) with the same columns; outputs go to
`outnew-N.tsv` with the same format. They hold only words that have no level yet. Rate each word as a
learner meets it in its most common meaning; judge every row with the same care from the first to the
last.

## Review instruction (summary)

The review agent read the level descriptions above and checked each row with lemma, part of speech,
gloss, frequency rank and proposed level; it kept the proposal unless clearly off by at least one
step, with guidance for proper nouns (well-known names early, obscure names C2, rate the sense the
entry describes) and abbreviations. It wrote a reason of up to 8 words for every change.
