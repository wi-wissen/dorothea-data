## Instruction for the bulk agents (verbatim)

# Run es-level-2026-10-22-a: CEFR levels for Spanish headwords

You assign a CEFR level (A1, A2, B1, B2, C1, C2) to Spanish dictionary headwords. The level answers:
**at which stage does a learner of Spanish as a foreign language (for example a German-speaking school
student who learns Spanish as a second or third foreign language) typically learn and need this word?**
Work from your own knowledge of Spanish and of how Spanish is learned. Do not look up or reproduce any
published word list (exam lists, textbook lists, Plan curricular del Instituto Cervantes, vocabulary
profiles, ELELex), and do not open any other files than the ones named below.

## Levels (own descriptions)

- **A1** – first weeks: greetings, numbers (all numbers up to 100 are A1), family, food, the home,
  time, colours, the most frequent verbs and adjectives, and all core function words (articles,
  pronouns, common prepositions and conjunctions, question words, ser/estar/tener/haber, common modal
  uses like poder/querer/deber).
- **A2** – everyday routine: shopping, doctor, appointments, transport, school, simple work, weather,
  feelings, describing people and past events; frequent concrete nouns and common verbs.
- **B1** – independent everyday use: opinions and reasons, work and training, media, health in more
  detail, travel problems, the news in simple terms; common abstract nouns; frequent connectors.
- **B2** – abstract and specialised topics of general interest: society, politics, economy,
  environment, science in general terms; everyday news and civic vocabulary (tax, budget, court,
  election, municipal, sector); general formal verbs; formal letters.
- **C1** – precise, educated vocabulary for study and work: academic and administrative words, nuanced
  or less frequent synonyms, literary words that are still in use, idiomatic and stylistically marked
  words.
- **C2** – only truly rare, archaic, regional or narrowly technical words that even advanced learners
  meet only occasionally.

## Calibration (learned from an earlier run for another language — follow it)

The earlier run was systematically **too strict** for frequent words. Avoid these errors:
1. **Rate the word, not the gloss.** The gloss shows up to three senses and may start with a rare
   one. Rate the word as a learner meets it in its most common meaning in this part of speech
   (e.g. a basic verb is A1 even if the first gloss shows a legal sense).
2. Everyday concrete words (places in town, food, clothes, school, technology like móvil, internet,
   correo) are A1–A2, not B1+.
3. News, civic and economic vocabulary is **B2**, not C1. General formal verbs (realizar, establecer,
   llevar a cabo, desarrollar) are B1–B2.
4. Frequent connectors and fixed-phrase words (sin embargo, además, a pesar de, en cuanto a, por lo
   tanto) are B1–B2 according to how early learners need them.
5. Transparent derivations sit at or one step above their base word (rápidamente ≈ rápido).
6. Words that live in a frequent fixed phrase get the level of the phrase (de repente → repente B1).
7. C2 is **not** the default for "uncommon": formal or literary words that educated texts still use
   are C1.
8. Also catch the opposite: academic or technical terms are not A2 just because they are short.
9. Nationality words of well-known countries A2–B1; of smaller regions B2–C1; town-inhabitant names
   C2 or `-`.

Further guidance:
- Derived words and compounds: the level of the whole word.
- Colloquial and slang words: the level at which learners understand them, typically B1–B2 for very
  common coloquial words (vale, guay, chaval), C1+ for argot. Regional (Latin American or Spanish)
  everyday words that are standard in their region are rated like other everyday words.
- Use the whole scale. Do not default to B1 when unsure; decide, and mark `unsure` instead.
- `cefr` may be `-` when the row is not a word a learner needs a level for: a name filed as a common
  word, a fragment, an abbreviation that is not used as a word, a foreign word that is not Spanish.

## Instruction for the reviews (verbatim; the last section was added for review 2)

# Review: Spanish CEFR levels of less frequent words

Read BRIEF.md in this directory first (scale, calibration, rules). The rows below were rated by a
smaller model; check them. Rate the word as a learner meets it in its most common meaning; the gloss
may show a rare sense. freq_rank is the lemma's rank in a large Spanish corpus (1 = most frequent),
one signal among others (ranks can be inflated by homographs or names). Remember: C2 only for truly
rare, archaic, regional or narrowly technical words; formal or literary words still used in educated
texts are C1; everyday words are A1–B1 even when not frequent in written text.

Do not consult published level lists (ELELex, Plan curricular etc.). Judge row by row yourself;
scripts only to validate. Output: review-K.out.tsv with header `id	lemma	pos	current	new	note`, one
row per input row in input order; note empty when unchanged, else a few English words.

## Findings of the first review (apply them)

A sample review changed about half of these rows, almost all downward: C2 was used as the default for
anything uncommon (formal, legal, administrative, literary words still in use → C1); transparent
-mente adverbs, -able adjectives and ex-/tele-/mini-/super- forms sat far above their base word; news,
sports and civic words were C1/C2 instead of B2; words living in a common idiom were rated by a rare
sense; basic food, classroom and daily-routine words were A2 instead of A1; adjectives for religions,
historical peoples and regions got `-` although they are real words (C1–C2). About 40 % of the C2
ratings were right (archaic, narrowly technical, rare regional) — keep those.
