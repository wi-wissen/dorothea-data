## Instruction for the bulk agents (verbatim)

# Run la-level-2026-10-24-a: school levels for Latin headwords

You assign a level to Latin dictionary headwords. Latin is not learned with the CEFR, so Dorothea
uses its six level slots for the **stages of Latin at a German secondary school** (Latin as second or
third foreign language, textbook phase followed by reading original authors). The level answers:
**at which stage does a pupil typically learn and need this word?**
Work from your own knowledge of Latin and of how Latin is taught. Do not look up or reproduce any
published word list (Bamberger Wortschatz, adeo, textbook vocabularies, Lektüre-Grundwortschätze,
frequency dictionaries), and do not open any files other than the ones named below.

## Levels (own descriptions)

- **A1** – first school year of Latin: the most frequent words and all core function words (sum,
  possum, et, sed, in, ad, cum, non, is/ea/id, hic, ille, qui, quis, ego, tu, se, suus, omnis,
  magnus, bonus, multus, res, homo, puer, puella, amare, videre, dicere, facere, esse, ire, numbers
  one to ten, frequent everyday and family words).
- **A2** – second school year: further frequent verbs, nouns and adjectives of the textbook
  stories (Roman everyday life, history and myth: urbs, templum, deus, bellum, miles, hostis, rex,
  imperium, mos, virtus, iubere, petere, quaerere, relinquere), frequent adverbs and conjunctions
  (tamen, enim, igitur, quia, postquam, dum).
- **B1** – end of the textbook phase and transition reading (Nepos, Phaedrus, easy Caesar): words a
  pupil is expected to know when starting original texts (consilium, auxilium, proelium, legio,
  castra, potestas, cognoscere, constituere, proficisci, quamquam, quoniam, ceterum).
- **B2** – core reading vocabulary of the school authors (Caesar, Cicero, Ovid, Catullus, Seneca,
  Pliny): words that recur in these texts and are learned during reading (deditio, munitio, oratio
  in its rhetorical sense, perfidia, exsilium, perturbare, insidiae).
- **C1** – wider reading vocabulary: words of poetry (Vergil, Ovid's Metamorphoses, Horace), Livy,
  Tacitus, philosophical Cicero, that a good pupil reads with a dictionary (aequor, nemus, ensis,
  cruor, sidus in poetic use, livor).
- **C2** – rare words: technical, archaic, Greek loans of narrow use, words found mainly in late
  antique, Christian or medieval Latin, words a pupil practically never meets.

## Calibration (learned from earlier runs for other languages — follow it)

Earlier runs were systematically **too strict** for frequent words. Avoid these errors:
1. **Rate the word, not the gloss.** The gloss shows up to four senses (German and English) and
   may start with a rare one or with a grammatical description; rate the word in its most common
   meaning in this part of speech.
2. Frequent words of the school texts are A1–B1, not B2+. A word that occurs on almost every page
   of Caesar or Cicero is at most B1.
3. C2 is **not** the default for "uncommon": a word that the school authors use is at most C1.
4. Transparent derivations and compounds sit at or one step above their base word (perficere ≈
   facere + one step; celeriter ≈ celer).
5. Words that live in a frequent fixed phrase get the level of the phrase (res publica → publicus
   A2; gratias agere).
6. Also catch the opposite: a short word is not A1 just because it is short (ops, vis are frequent,
   but cos, fas, nex are not first-year words).
7. Late and medieval-only words (Christian vocabulary, scholastic terms) are C2 unless they are also
   classical. Very frequent Christian words that school readers still meet (deus, ecclesia in late
   texts) follow their classical use.
8. Several rows can share a lemma with different parts of speech (`et` conj and `et` adv, `cum`
   conj and `cum` prep, `qui` pron and `qui` det): rate each in its own use.

Further guidance:
- `-` when the row is not a word a pupil needs a level for: a misfiled form, a fragment, an
  abbreviation, a name filed as a common word (adjectives of place like Gallicus are words and get a
  level: frequent ones B1–B2, obscure ones C2).
- Use the whole scale. Do not default to B1 when unsure; decide, and mark `unsure` instead.
